## Executive Summary

Hope Cement Works (Breedon Group, Derbyshire) is one of four sites in the Peak Cluster carbon capture and storage (CCS) project — a proposed £5bn, ~198km pipeline to transport CO₂ from Peak District cement and lime operations to offshore storage under the Irish Sea. This analysis assesses how Hope can most cost-effectively prepare its CO₂ stream for that pipeline.

Breedon has publicly stated (March 2025 community update) that amine scrubbing — not oxy-fuel combustion — is "closest to realisation" for Hope, with disclosed internal cost estimates of approximately £400m for capture and £300m for CO₂ transport infrastructure. A public Environment Agency permit decision document confirms why: Hope's two kilns were installed in the early 1970s with a multistage cyclone preheater but no calciner, making a main-burner oxy-fuel retrofit a substantially harder and less-precedented undertaking than for a modern precalciner kiln. This analysis does not contest that decision. Instead, it models the smallest capital package that meaningfully reduces the running cost of the amine plant Breedon has already chosen: (1) routing kiln waste heat directly to the amine reboiler's steam demand, (2) a De-SOx pretreatment step to protect the solvent from Hope's unusually sulphur-rich local shale, and (3) battery storage (BESS) to buffer the pipeline-compression electrical load.

**Headline result:** an estimated £13.9m incremental capital package returns £6.6m/yr in operating cost savings — a simple payback of approximately **2.1 years**. Over the plant's modelled 20-year life at an 8% discount rate, that's a net present value of approximately **£51.3m** and an internal rate of return of approximately **48%** — the payback is the hook, the NPV is the more complete picture.

A Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis across a coupled, sourced set of inputs puts the payback range at 1.5–3.8 years (P10–P90), with the optimized package beating the as-planned baseline in 99.9% of 10,000 simulated scenarios. The result also holds up against the most obvious objection: because the same baseline amine-plant capex appears in both the "as-planned" and "optimized" cases, it cancels out of the comparison algebraically — recalculating at 20%, or even 50%, above Breedon's disclosed £400m leaves the £13.9m add-on's payback and savings unchanged to three decimal places.

Both the waste-heat capex line and the De-SOx cost structure have been rebuilt on real, sourced primary data, replacing two rounds of unbenchmarked placeholders. The remaining uncertainty is concentrated in a few disclosed, well-defined gaps: a ~10% figure — engineering judgment, not a sourced probability — that a standalone SOx polisher proves necessary beyond the standard pretreatment already assumed inside the £400m baseline; a waste-heat capex range drawn from new-build cost data that may understate what a 50-year-old kiln retrofit actually costs; and no vendor confirmation yet of Hope's real SOx tolerance.
